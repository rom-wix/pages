Cross-sell & upsell of additional services in cart and checkout

How 40+ booking platforms, service marketplaces, hospitality vendors and e-commerce checkouts let a customer add more services after they've chosen the first one — what they call it, where they put it, who configures it, and what it earns.

Prepared for Wix Bookings — cart & checkout upsell Date 18 August 2026 Platforms reviewed 44 Sources 190+ help articles, docs, filings and case studies

01What the market actually does

Five things are true across every category, and they contradict some common assumptions about putting an upsell in the checkout.

Products ≠ add-ons
Timely and Phorest explicitly exclude retail products from service add-ons; Fresha's add-ons are services-only in practice. Vagaro is the exception — everything lands in one cart.
A real fork in the product model
The headline tension. Half the category believes an in-cart upsell cannibalises booking conversion — Duve says so outright ("you don't want to distract travelers… let the travelers choose you first"), Bókun's "Upsell" deliberately creates a separate booking rather than touching the original, and Checkfront advises putting only low-consideration items at checkout. But the numbers don't support the fear: Revinate finds the post-purchase confirmation email out-earns pre-arrival email ($86 vs $82 per booking), and the airline bundle-grid test raised per-session value 13% while costing 1% of conversion. The resolution isn't "don't upsell at checkout" — it's put high-consideration items immediately after commitment, keep low-consideration items in the cart, and measure per-session value.

03The four placement schools

Strip away the branding and every implementation lands in one of four buckets. They are not equally good, and the choice is largely determined by whether your add-ons carry duration.

A · Interstitial step after service selection, before time (dominant)

Fresha, Timely, Phorest, Boulevard and SimplyBook.me all put a dedicated screen between "which service" and "when". Timely titles it "Enhance your experience" with explicit Add / Skip this step buttons. This exists because add-ons that add minutes must be known before availability is computed — SimplyBook.me documents the failure mode plainly: on templates where the add-on step comes after slot selection, an incompatible combination throws a booking error.

B · Inline on the service-selection screen

Acuity puts an "Add to Appointment" checkbox list directly above the calendar, on the same page. Lower friction, no extra step, but far less prominent — and it competes for attention with the calendar itself.

C · Cart / checkout stage

Vagaro is the only appointment platform with a real cart model: add-ons flow into the Shopping Cart and remain editable at the POS checkout screen. In tours & activities the pattern is more mature — FareHarbor's Combos interstitial fires after booking details, before payment details; Xola's XolaBot injects a modal after "Continue"; Rezdy deliberately puts upsells in the sticky right rail below the order total "so that the checkout flow is not impacted".

D · Post-purchase / post-booking

The entire hospitality vendor cohort lives here, and Bókun's "Upsell" goes furthest — it creates an entirely separate booking rather than modifying the original. Revinate's benchmark suggests this is not the consolation prize it looks like: the confirmation email produced $86 of upsell revenue per booking versus $82 from the pre-arrival email.

The pattern nobody uses, and why it matters. Almost no service-booking platform puts add-ons on the payment screen. Checkfront states the rule explicitly: at checkout, offer only low-consideration items — gratuity, small gear, parking — because "the guest has made their decision, so asking them to make another big one can increase abandonment." Airlines are the conspicuous exception, and they are currently under UK CMA investigation for it.

06What this implies for the feature

  • Decide the duration question first — it determines placement, not the other way round. If add-on services consume calendar time, the picker must come before slot selection, and you inherit the whole SimplyBook.me failure mode when it doesn't. If you want add-ons genuinely in the cart and at checkout, the cleanest path is Boulevard's and Phorest's: support 0-minute add-ons as a first-class type, and reserve those for the checkout surface.
  • Support all three quantity semantics from day one, because everyone retrofits them. Rezdy calls them Per Order / Per Quantity / Customer's choice; Bókun No maximum / Maximum by participant / Custom; Checkfront gets there via "inherit parent guest types"; Xola crosses Quantity/Checkbox/Dropdown with "ask organizer only / ask each attendee". Four independent products, same three modes.
  • Model an add-on as multiple offer moments, not one SKU with one price. This is the airline lesson and it's the single most under-copied idea in the service-booking category. Ryanair sells the same bag allowance at four different prices at four different moments. You don't need dark patterns to use the structure — but a data model where "offer" is separate from "product" is what makes a second, cheaper checkout-stage nudge possible at all.
  • Ship a template library, not an empty configuration screen. Oaky's Deal Library (100+ pre-built offers in 25+ languages) and ServiceTitan's proposal templates both exist because merchants don't reliably invent good add-ons. Given Wix's long tail of small businesses, the difference between "configure your add-ons" and "here are 12 add-ons that work for hair salons" is likely the difference between 5% and 50% merchant adoption.
  • Give the merchant a live guest-side preview. Duve's builder shows the merchant exactly what the client will see while they configure. Cheap to build, and it directly addresses the trust problem of a merchant enabling something that appears in their own checkout.
  • Copy Klook's cascading "(Optional)" selector with an explicit None default. It is the most directly transferable client-facing pattern found — it makes skipping a first-class, visible choice rather than an omission, which is both better UX and safer ground given the CMA's current drip-pricing scrutiny.
  • Do not pre-check boxes. Checkfront names this as an anti-pattern that "can burn trust… may run afoul of consumer protection rules." SimplyBook.me and Bókun both permit pre-selection, and FareHarbor recommends auto-selecting trip protection — but the regulatory direction of travel is clearly against it. Pre-highlighting (ServiceTitan's "Recommended" flag, Jobber's recommended assortment) achieves most of the lift without the exposure.
  • Decide early whether products can be add-ons. Timely ("Can I offer product add-ons? Not at this stage") and Phorest ("Retail Add-ons are currently not supported") both rule it out explicitly; Fresha's are services-only in practice. Vagaro doesn't rule it out, and that's precisely why it has a real cart. If the goal is "more services in cart and checkout" you can start services-only — but the cart abstraction you build now determines whether products can join later without a rewrite.
  • Constrain the add-on taxonomy if distribution matters. GetYourGuide's six fixed categories are what let Regiondo and TourCMS auto-map inventory in. Free-text types make every future integration bespoke.
  • Instrument per-session value, not conversion rate. The one real A/B test in this research would have been killed by a conversion-rate guardrail while creating 13% more value per session. Set the success metric before shipping the experiment.

Nobody is doing this — the open space

Across 15 appointment-booking platforms, zero use algorithmic recommendation. Every implementation is a merchant-curated per-service list. Zenoti's "Realtime Recos" is the only feature name that even gestures at it, and its documentation could not be retrieved. Meanwhile the e-commerce cohort (Rebuy, Zipify) has been running recommendation engines against cart contents for years and publishes 10–27% AOV lifts for it. If there's a differentiated position available in service-booking upsell, "the add-ons suggest themselves, correctly, without the merchant configuring anything" is it.

07Research gaps

Honest accounting of what couldn't be verified, and why. Most of these are blocked by client-rendered help centres that return empty pages to automated fetching — they resolve with a browser session, not more searching.

  • Urban Company — the highest-value remaining gap. Anecdotally the most aggressive in-cart service recommender in the market; site and help centre are fully client-rendered. Only signal recovered: it merchandises "Bundles". Needs a manual walkthrough of the app.
  • Mindbody, Zenoti, GlossGenius, Treatwell — add-on features confirmed to exist, mechanics unverifiable. Salesforce / Paligo / Elevio portals all return loading shells. Zenoti is the most interesting of the four given its "Realization" revenue framing.
  • Square Appointments — no add-on capability found on the product overview, but the help centre resisted access. Treat as unconfirmed rather than a "No".
  • Booking.com partner side — room-upgrade and property-configured "extra services" mechanics on the extranet. partner.booking.com returns an empty body on every fetch.
  • Thumbtack, Angi, TaskRabbit, Rover, Amazon — all client-rendered or actively blocking. Rover's help centre returned zero matches for "add-on", so the feature may exist under another name.
  • Peek Pro and FareHarbor help-centre internals — both sit behind SSO/sign-in walls; the public blog and product pages were the only accessible sources.

To close these: a browser session against the six client-rendered help centres, plus a manual walkthrough of Urban Company, Booksy and Mindbody's live consumer booking flows, would fill essentially every remaining gap. That's roughly half a day of work and would add the client-facing screenshots this report is thinnest on.
