Most "oil trading bots" sold to retail traders are grids, martingales or averaging-down systems. We simulated typical settings with the conditions a retail CFD account really has: 1:10 leverage (the EU limit for commodity CFDs), a broker stop-out at a 50% margin level, spreads, financing and roll yield. We opened a new account every month from 1991 and ran each one for two years.

Why they look good at first

Every closed grid and averaging-down trade is a winner and equity climbs smoothly, because losing positions are never closed. The longest-lived grid ran six years on Brent, reached 3.5× its starting balance with 123 winning trades, and was wiped out in the December 2014 collapse. Left to run, 91–100% of grid and averaging-down accounts were eventually ruined, after a median of 0.5–1.4 years.

When they died

Grid and averaging-down accounts were stopped out in September 2008, November 2014 and on 6 March 2020 in crude, and in August 2008 in gas. One averaging-down account nearly doubled in the 2021–22 gas spike, then died in January 2023. By the day WTI futures went negative in April 2020, they had already been liquidated weeks earlier.

No safe setting

We tried 72 settings per bot. Averaging down never had a positive average two-year return. The safest grid still had a 13% chance of losing half, in exchange for about +5% over two years. Remove the martingale's doubling and the same entries become harmless (Sharpe 0.10–0.37, a 0–1% chance of losing half), so the doubling itself does the damage.
