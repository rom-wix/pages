Product recommendation

Cross-selling services in cart and checkout

What to build, in what order, and why — grounded in a 44-platform competitive benchmark and in what Wix Bookings already ships today. Scope is services only: recommending other bookable services, not products or plans.

Scope Services only Shape MVP + roadmap Companion 44-platform market research Date 24 August 2026

01Start here: the feature you're describing isn't add-ons

Wix Bookings shipped per-service Add-Ons in 2025. Before designing anything, it's worth being precise about what that already covers and what it doesn't — because the gap is narrower and more interesting than "we need add-ons".

CapabilityStatus todayDetail
Per-service priced extras Ships Add-Ons: reusable add-ons organised into per-service groups. Max 3 groups × 7 add-ons for appointments, 1 group for classes. Each carries a price and either additional time or quantity — not both. Shown before the client picks a time, with duration and cost folded into the displayed total.
Several services, one appointment Ships Multi-service appointments: client picks a service, taps + Add Service, up to 5. Same date and location, sessions run back-to-back, availability computed as the intersection. Client picks staff per service — same-staff-for-all is an open request.
Services + products, one checkout Ships The Bookings cart is the shared Wix eCommerce cart. Bookings and Stores check out together.
Recommending other services Missing + Add Service is a blank button. Nothing suggests which service. Add-Ons only surface extras attached to a service the client already chose.
Anything at the cart stage Missing Once a booking is in the cart it is inert. Add-ons can't be edited there — though the Add-Ons API explicitly lists "modify add-ons in eCommerce carts" as an intended use case, so the platform already anticipates this.
Anything at the checkout step Missing No native cross-sell block — but 10 checkout site-plugin slots exist, including checkout:summary:lineItems:after and checkout:summary:totalsBreakdown:before. The anchors are already there.
Post-purchase offer Missing Thank-You Page and Side Cart slots exist. Nothing uses them for Bookings.
Curated bundles Missing "Custom service bundles are not currently available" — open feature request.
So the real gap is three things, not one: (1) nothing recommends a service — the merchant configures extras, but the platform never suggests a service the client didn't think of; (2) the cart and checkout are dead surfaces where competitors put their second and third offer; (3) there's no post-purchase offer at all, which the benchmark data says is the highest-return, lowest-risk surface of the lot.

There is also an existing third-party app — Service Upsell Guru — doing a narrow version of this: merchant maps trigger services to upsell services, and the app renders them in an "Added to Cart Modal". Notably it only offers services available within 2 hours of the selected slot. That constraint is not an implementation shortcut. It's the whole problem, and it's the subject of the next section.

02Why services are hard to add at checkout

Every e-commerce cross-sell pattern assumes the added item needs no decisions. A service breaks that assumption, and this single fact explains almost every design choice in the competitive set.

Adding a product to a cart is one click. Adding a service requires resolving four things: a staff member, a date, a time, and availability that still exists at the moment of payment. That's not an upsell — that's a second booking flow, and you'd be asking the client to run it while their card is half-entered.

This is why the dominant pattern across appointment platforms is an interstitial after service selection and before time-slot selection — Fresha, Timely, Phorest, Boulevard and SimplyBook.me all put it there. It's the last moment where you can change the duration of the appointment without invalidating a choice the client has already made. SimplyBook.me documents the failure mode explicitly: on templates where the add-on step falls after time selection, an incompatible combination throws a booking error at the client.

The four mechanics that actually work

Across the whole benchmark, only four ways of adding a service late in the flow hold up. They are not equally good and they belong on different surfaces.

#MechanicHow it worksWhere it can safely live
A Extend the appointment
Recommended MVP		 The suggested service is appended back-to-back to the booking already in the cart — same date, same location, immediately after the existing end time. Reuses the multi-service appointment machinery that already ships. Only offered if the following block is genuinely free. The client makes zero new scheduling decisions; they see a new end time. Cart · Checkout summary
B Nearby-slot booking The service is offered at a slot near — but not adjacent to — the existing booking. Client accepts one pre-computed option ("2:30pm, right after your cut"). This is what Service Upsell Guru does with its 2-hour window. Broader reach than A, but it introduces a real scheduling decision, so it's a worse fit for the payment step. Cart · Booking flow
C Zero-duration service
Checkout-safe		 A service that consumes no calendar time and therefore needs no slot at all — patch test, aftercare kit fulfilled at the visit, digital follow-up, gratuity-style items. Boulevard and Phorest both make 0-minute services a first-class type precisely so they can be sold late. This is the only category that belongs on the payment screen. Checkout · Post-purchase
D Separate booking The added service becomes its own booking rather than joining the original. Bókun does exactly this — its post-purchase "Upsell" sends the customer into a fresh checkout and explicitly does not modify the original reservation. Zero risk to the primary conversion, but it's a new transaction. Post-purchase only
The rule that makes all four safe: silently filter, never fail. Compute real availability before deciding whether to render the offer. An offer that can't be fulfilled must simply not appear. The inverse lesson is Checkfront's, which is severe by design: if a required upsell is unavailable, Checkfront makes the parent product unbookable. That's the right instinct applied to a required item — and the same instinct applied to an optional one means: don't show it.
Do not put a duration-carrying service on the payment screen. It is the one thing the benchmark is unanimous about. Checkfront states the rule outright — at checkout, offer only low-consideration items (gratuity, small gear, parking), because "the guest has made their decision, so asking them to make another big one can increase abandonment." Airlines are the conspicuous exception, and they are currently under UK CMA investigation for drip pricing.

03What goes on each surface

Four offer moments, each with a different job. The mistake to avoid is treating them as one feature with a placement setting — they carry different mechanics, different limits and different risk.

1 · Booking flowAfter service, before time
Highest-value services go here. Today + Add Service is a blank button; make it a recommendation. Show 2–3 suggested services alongside it, with price and added duration, and keep Skip as a visible first-class action (Timely titles this screen "Enhance your experience" and pairs Add with Skip this step).

This is the only surface where a service of any duration is safe, because the slot hasn't been chosen yet. It should carry your most valuable recommendations, not your leftovers.
2 · CartBefore checkout begins
"Add to your booking" block. Mechanic A (extend) and C (zero-duration). Max 2–3 offers. Each shows the resulting new end time inline — "adds 20 min · your appointment now ends 3:20pm" — so the consequence is visible before the tap.

Also the natural home for editing add-ons already in the cart, which the Add-Ons API already names as an intended use case. That's the cheapest possible first win.
3 · Checkout summaryPayment step
Zero-duration only. Max 1–2. Secondary buttons only. Anchor to checkout:summary:lineItems:after — Shopify's equivalent guidance is to sit the offer just outside the order summary so line items and money lines stay scannable together, and to treat the offer as secondary content.

No success toast: the summary updating is the confirmation. Watch the collapsed-summary problem on mobile — Shopify documents that a collapsed order summary hides the offer entirely until expanded.
4 · Thank-you + emailPost-purchase
The highest-return, lowest-risk surface, and you have nothing here today. Revinate's 2026 benchmark found the confirmation email produced $86 of upsell revenue per booking versus $82 from the pre-arrival email — post-purchase out-earns the "better" moment. And because the sale is already banked, there is no conversion risk at all.

Mechanic A (extend, if the slot is still free) or D (separate booking). This is also where "book your next appointment" belongs, which is a retention lever as much as a revenue one.
If you only ship two surfaces, ship 1 and 4. Surface 1 is where the money is and the machinery already half-exists. Surface 4 costs you nothing in conversion risk and the benchmark says it out-earns the surfaces everyone obsesses over. The cart and checkout blocks are the interesting design work, but they are not where the first dollar comes from.

04Where to recommend from — the actual opportunity

Across 15 appointment-booking platforms, zero use algorithmic recommendation. Every single one is a merchant-curated per-service list. Meanwhile the e-commerce cohort has been running cart-content recommenders for years and publishes 10–27% AOV lifts for it. That gap is the most defensible thing in this whole space.

V1  Merchant-curated pairs

Merchant links services to services, the way Boulevard, Timely and Phorest do. Extend the existing Add-Ons manager rather than building a second config surface — merchants should not have to learn two mental models for "things that get suggested".

Table stakes. Ships the feature. Differentiates nothing.

V2  Learn from the merchant's own history

"Clients who booked a colour also booked a treatment within 30 days." Plain co-occurrence over the site's own booking history — no ML required, fully explainable to the merchant, and it works from day one on any site with reasonable volume.

This is the zero-of-fifteen gap. It is also the answer to the real adoption problem: most small merchants will never curate a good pairing list.

V3  Vertical benchmarks across Wix

"Salons like yours pair these two." Cross-site co-occurrence by vertical, used to seed sites with too little history for V2 — the cold-start solution.

No competitor can do this. Fresha and Booksy have marketplace scale but not Wix's breadth of verticals; Boulevard and Phorest have neither. This is the part that is genuinely hard to copy.

Always leave the merchant an override. Every algorithmic suggestion should be visible in the merchant dashboard as a concrete pairing they can pin, edit or kill — not a black box. The reason is commercial, not technical: a merchant who doesn't trust the suggestions turns the feature off, and you never get the data to improve it.

The adoption lever nobody talks about: starter packs

Oaky ships a Deal Library of 100+ pre-built offers in 25+ languages so the merchant never configures an upsell from a blank page. ServiceTitan ships proposal templates for the same reason. Given Wix's long tail of very small businesses, the difference between "configure your cross-sells" and "here are 8 pairings that work for hair salons" is plausibly the difference between single-digit and majority merchant adoption. Ship vertical packs — hair, nails, beauty, massage & spa, personal training, pet grooming, photography, tutoring, home services — with the feature, not after it.

One more merchant-side idea worth stealing: Duve's config builder shows the merchant a live guest-side preview while they configure. Cheap to build, and it directly addresses the hesitation of switching on something that appears in your own checkout.

05Guardrails

These are drawn from published platform limits and from convergent practice across independent vendors — not from taste. Where four unrelated companies land on the same number, that number is doing real work.

06Phasing

Ordered by value-per-unit-of-risk, not by ambition. Each phase is independently shippable and independently measurable.

Phase 0

Make add-ons editable in the cart

Smallest useful thing

The Add-Ons API already names "modify add-ons in eCommerce carts" as an intended use case, so the platform has anticipated this. It delivers a literal reading of "more to add in cart" with no new concepts, no recommendation logic and no scheduling risk — and it establishes the cart as a live surface, which every later phase depends on.

07How to measure it

Get this wrong and you will kill a winning feature. It has already happened elsewhere in the benchmark.

The cautionary tale. The one genuine A/B test in the entire research — an airline bundle grid versus an à-la-carte page — lost 1% of conversion while gaining 14% AOV and 13% per-session value. Under a conversion-rate guardrail it would have been switched off. Agree the primary metric and the conversion tolerance before the experiment runs, not after you see the numbers.
MetricRoleNotes
Revenue per booking sessionPrimaryPer-session value, not per-order. This is the metric that would have saved the airline test.
Attach rate by surfaceDiagnosticSegment by surface and by mechanic. Benchmarks to aim at: ~16% take rate is where two independent e-commerce vendors land on post-purchase; 5–12% is the hospitality pre-arrival range; Peek Pro claims ~9% order-revenue lift from in-flow add-ons.
Cart → order completionGuardrailNot the primary metric. Set an explicit tolerance up front — a small, funded conversion cost may be the correct trade.
Offer suppression rateHealthHow often an offer is computed but withheld for unavailability. High rates mean the pairing logic is fighting the calendar and Phase 3's mechanic A needs loosening toward B.
Merchant adoptionLeadingShare of eligible sites with ≥1 pairing configured. If starter packs work, this moves first and everything else follows. If it stalls, no downstream metric will save the feature.
Rebook rateSecondarySpecific to Phase 2. The post-purchase surface is partly a retention lever and shouldn't be judged on AOV alone.

08What not to build

  • Duration-carrying services on the payment screen. The single clearest finding in the research. Restrict the checkout step to zero-duration items and you keep the surface without the abandonment risk or the slot-validation nightmare.
  • A second configuration surface. Cross-sell pairings belong inside the existing Add-Ons manager. Two places to configure "things that get suggested" is how a feature dies of merchant confusion.
  • Bidding or dynamic pricing. UpsellGuru's bid-for-upgrade and Oracle Nor1's perishable pricing work because a hotel room is perishable inventory with a marginal cost near zero. A stylist's hour is not the same asset, and the mechanic doesn't transfer.
  • Cross-merchant cross-sell. FareHarbor's Cross-Sell Combos and Bókun's Marketplace upsell work because those are supplier networks. A Wix site is one business. Recommending another merchant's service inside a merchant's own checkout is a different — and much larger — product decision.
  • A "we'll schedule it later" credit. It removes all the slot math, which is why it's tempting. It also creates an unfulfilled obligation, a support surface, and a refund question. If you want deferred value, that's a packages/plans feature, not a cross-sell one.
  • Aggressive sequencing. The airline pattern — selling the same item 3–5 times at different prices across the funnel — genuinely works and is genuinely under-copied. It is also exactly what the UK CMA is currently investigating Ryanair for. The structural idea worth taking is that offer and product should be separate objects, so a second, cheaper nudge is possible later. Taking the frequency with it is not worth the exposure.

09Open questions for the team

  1. Do zero-duration services exist as a first-class type today? Phase 3's checkout block depends on it entirely. Boulevard and Phorest both treat 0-minute services as a distinct type precisely so they can be sold late in the flow. If Wix doesn't have it, that's the prerequisite ticket.
  2. Does a held cart hold the slot? Everything hinges on this. If the slot isn't reserved while the client is in checkout, mechanic A needs re-validation at payment and a graceful failure path. GetYourGuide's reservation hold defaults to 60 minutes — worth knowing what Wix's equivalent is.
  3. Is the 5-service ceiling on multi-service appointments a hard constraint? Phase 1 recommendations sit under it. Not necessarily a problem, but it should be a deliberate limit rather than an inherited one.
  4. Native mobile apps. Add-ons, multi-service and the cart are all documented as unavailable in the Wix mobile apps. If a meaningful share of bookings come through those, the roadmap needs a parallel track — or an explicit decision to accept web-only.
  5. Build native, or extend Service Upsell Guru's model? A third-party app already occupies part of this space. Worth a deliberate call on whether the native feature supersedes it, and what that means for merchants already using it.
  6. Do you want the multi-service same-staff constraint fixed first? Clients currently pick staff per service. For a cross-sell like "add a blow-dry after your cut", "same person, right after" is very likely the offer people actually want — and it's an open feature request today.
  7. Custom booking forms. Multi-service appointments currently fall back to the default form. If Phase 1 pushes more bookings down the multi-service path, more merchants lose their custom forms as a side effect.
