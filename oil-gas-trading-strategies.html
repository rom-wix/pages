Energy Impulse Playbook

What happens after a strong move in oil or gas, once price pauses and builds a range? We tested 13 ways to trade that pause on 1-hour, 4-hour and daily bars and found one that is worth trading.

Backtests on WTI and natural gas 1-minute data (2005 to April 2020) and WTI, Brent and Henry Hub daily closes (1986 to September 2026). All results are net of spread, commission and stop slippage. R = the amount risked on a trade (entry to stop).

The short answer

Trade the continuation, on daily bars, in crude oil

After a strong 4 to 6 day move, wait for a 4 to 6 day pause that stays tight. Then trade the break of that pause in the direction of the original move. Take half the profit at the measured move and trail the rest. Only take the setup when volatility is above its one-year median.

  • Continuation beats retracement on crude. On daily oil charts the pause breaks with the impulse first 53 to 57% of the time, and continuation trades earned +0.35R to +0.75R each after costs on all three oil datasets. Trades against the impulse were less reliable: +0.06R to +0.51R without the filter, and −0.11R on the longest WTI record with it.
  • Intraday versions don't survive costs. On 1-hour and 4-hour bars the pause breaks against the impulse about as often as with it, or more often, and none of the 13 approaches stayed positive after costs in both test periods.
  • Natural gas has no edge from this pattern. Every variant lost money in at least one decade. We don't recommend trading gas this way.
  • It is a slow, lumpy system. About 2 to 4 trades a year per market, around half of them winners, and the three best trades made roughly half to two-thirds of all the profit. Skipping signals is the fastest way to lose the edge.

System 1

The rules

Daily bars on the CME session (18:00 to 17:00 New York). For WTI (CL, MCL, USOIL) or Brent (BZ, UKOIL). Every distance is in ATR, the 20-day average true range.

Oil flag breakout · dailyVersion A+
IMPULSE
A net move over the last 4, 5 or 6 days of at least 1.5 × ATR. Measure ATR on the day before the move started.
PAUSE
The next 4, 5 or 6 bars form a box. Box height at most 50% of the impulse, and the box gives back no more than 61.8% of it.
FILTER
ATR ÷ price is above its median of the past 252 days. Otherwise skip the setup. Without this rule you get version A: twice the trades at half the edge.
ENTRY
Buy stop at the box high after an up-impulse, sell stop at the box low after a down-impulse. Good for 10 trading days. Cancel it if the other side of the box breaks first.
STOP
The opposite edge of the box, never closer than 0.5 × ATR. Typically 1.2 to 1.5 × ATR away.
EXIT ½
Target = entry level ± one impulse length (the measured move). If not reached in 20 trading days, close at the market.
EXIT ½
Trail a stop 3.5 × ATR behind the daily close, moving only in your favour. Close after 60 trading days at most.
SIZE
Risk 0.5 to 1% of the account from entry to stop. One position per market. A WTI and a Brent signal on the same day count as one trade: split the risk.
Worked example, WTI, January 2026 (daily closes, EIA spot)
WTI rallied from $56.01 on 7 January to $61.84 on 14 January, a $5.83 impulse. The next five sessions closed between $59.13 and $60.38, a range of 21% of the impulse. That put the entry at $60.38 and the stop at $59.13: $1.25 per barrel of planned risk, or $125 per MCL contract. Trading on closes, the entry filled at $60.71 on 23 January.
The measured-move target was $60.38 + $5.83 = $66.21. The first half closed on 19 February at $66.66 (+4.8R). The trailing half rode the March supply shock and was stopped on 17 April at $85.90 (+20.1R). Net +12.4R, the kind of trade that pays for a year of small losses.
The core question

With the impulse or against it?

We took every impulse-and-pause setup the rules detect and traded it both ways: a stop order on the impulse side of the box (continuation) and on the opposite side (retracement), with identical stops, exits and costs. All setups, 2005 onward.

On daily crude charts the pause usually resolves with the impulse, and trading that side paid on all three oil datasets. The retracement side paid on two of the three, less reliably, and not at all on the longest WTI record once the volatility filter is applied (−0.11R). On 4-hour and 1-hour bars the box breaks against the impulse about as often as with it, or more often, and neither side covers its costs. On gas neither side works.

System 1 results

How the system performed

Applies to every chart and table in this section and the next two.

The edge is concentrated after 2005. Before that, on daily closes, the same rules lost money in WTI and made a modest profit in Brent. A likely reason: the oil market of the late 1980s and 1990s traded mostly inside an OPEC-managed range that rewarded fading moves, and it began to trend much harder once financial participation grew in the mid-2000s. We treat the pre-2005 result as a warning that this edge can fade, not as noise.

Two datasets, two execution styles. The 1-minute WTI series tests the rules as written, with resting stop orders, and its average loss is 0.96R. The daily-close series cover 40 years but enter and exit on the close. On the same 2005–2020 WTI data, acting on closes made more per trade (+1.24R against +0.66R with the filter), but losses averaged about 1.5R because the exit waits for the close. Both styles work. Resting stops keep each loss near the planned amount. If you prefer to act on closes, size every trade about a third smaller.

Stress tests

Robustness checks

What the checks say. The edge survives every parameter choice we mapped and costs up to four times retail CFD spreads. With the filter on, 0 to 2.5% of random-timing runs did as well as the real system; without it, 14% did on the 1-minute WTI data, so the filter carries much of the timing edge there. Two-sided entries and impulse-free boxes both earn less per trade, so the strong move before the pause matters. It isn't just a volatility-squeeze effect. The bootstrap range is wide, because a few very large winners dominate. With 34 trades the lower bound for WTI with 1-minute fills sits just above zero, so treat +0.66R as an estimate, not a promise.

What did not help. A trend filter (trade only in the direction of the 100-day average) helped on one dataset and hurt on another, so it is not part of the rules. The same rules on 4-hour bars made +0.38R per trade in 2005–2014 but only +0.05R in 2015–April 2020, so we list that version as experimental.

Henry Hub & NYMEX natural gas

Natural gas: no trade

The same rules lose money on natural gas: −0.10R per trade on NG futures data (2005 to April 2020) and −0.36R on Henry Hub daily closes since 2005. The 2005–2014 decade kills every gas variant we tried. That was the decade of the post-Katrina spike, the 2008 peak near $13 and the shale-driven collapse. Gas spikes are more often weather-driven and short-lived, and the front-month contango drags on multi-day longs.

The only gas variant with a positive recent record trades the box break against the impulse in winter, November to March. It made +0.27R per trade in 2015–2020 on futures data and +0.25R in 2020–2026 on Henry Hub closes, but lost −0.40R and −0.71R per trade in 2005–2014. We would not trade it with real money.

Practical

Running it

  1. After each daily close (17:00 New York), check WTI and Brent for a finished 4 to 6 day pause after a 1.5-ATR move. scan.py in the repository does this for any daily CSV and prints the order levels.
  2. Check the filter. Is today's ATR ÷ price above its median of the past year? If not, skip the setup.
  3. Place both orders at once: the entry stop at the box edge and, once filled, the protective stop at the opposite edge. Size so that a stop-out costs 0.5 to 1% of the account.
  4. On a fill, set the measured-move target for half the position. After each close, move the other half's stop to 3.5 × ATR behind the close if that is tighter.
  5. Cancel the entry after 10 trading days, or as soon as the other side of the box breaks.
  6. Log every signal, taken or not. Compare against the numbers below every 10 trades.

Instruments. Micro WTI (MCL, 100 barrels) keeps position sizes workable for smaller accounts. On CL or BZ, use a back-adjusted continuous chart so roll gaps don't create fake impulses, and roll positions before first notice. CFDs (USOIL, UKOIL) work too, but overnight financing includes the contango cost of rolling, which adds up over a 3 to 5 week hold.

Scaling. One market at 1% risk is a slow system: the median simulated decade returns about 1.5 to 4% a year. Risking 2% roughly doubles both return and drawdown. WTI and Brent move together, so trading both adds few independent trades. The better lever is applying the same rules to more markets, which needs its own testing.

How we know

Data and method

Data. This environment blocks Yahoo Finance, FRED, the EIA website and Stooq, so every series came from public GitHub repositories:

  • OANDA WTI and natural gas CFD 1-minute bars, 2005 to April 2020 (FutureSharks/financial-data). They are carry-adjusted cash prices, so there are no roll gaps; we checked this against EIA spot. The data is cut on 15 April 2020, before the negative-price dislocation.
  • EIA daily spot closes: WTI from 1986, Brent from 1987 (datasets/oil-prices), Henry Hub from 1997 (datasets/natural-gas), all to 22 September 2026.
  • USOIL and UKOIL CFD 1-hour and 1-minute samples for March to September 2026 (getdata-finance).

Engine. Signals use completed bars only; orders go live on the next bar. Every order is simulated on the 1-minute path where it exists, so a stop and a target inside the same daily bar are resolved in the order they actually happened. Gaps fill at the open. Limit orders need the price to trade through. The protective stop is checked first on the entry bar. One position at a time per market.

Testing. Families and parameters were chosen on 2005–2014. 2015–April 2020 was the first check, and the May 2020–September 2026 daily closes were kept as a holdout. The volatility filter and the 4–6 day ranges also win on 2005–2014 alone. More than 30,000 configurations were run in total, so we judge approaches by how consistent they are across settings, periods and datasets, not by the best single result.

Limits. The realistic 1-minute test covers only WTI and only to 2020, with 34 trades in version A+. Brent and post-2020 WTI rely on closing prices. EIA spot is not a traded price, and OANDA's CFD is not the futures contract. Before trading real money, rerun the tests on your broker's continuous CL and BZ futures history. scan.py --csv your_file.csv reruns the backtest on any daily OHLC file and lists live setups.

Reproduce. Code, sweeps and results are in oil-gas-strategies/ in this repository: data_build.py builds the bars, ogslib.py is the engine (its tests are in test_engine.py), system.py holds the rules and validate.py produces every number on this page.
